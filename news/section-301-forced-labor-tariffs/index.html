Since 24 July, most imports carry a 10% or 12.5% forced-labor tariff

The Section 301 forced-labor tariffs replaced the expired Section 122 surcharge, which had itself replaced the IEEPA tariffs the Supreme Court struck down. This is what now applies to imported components, and how IEEPA refunds work.

Published 29 Oct 2026 · Aliwal Robotics

Key dates

  • 20 Feb 2026Supreme Court rules 6–3 that IEEPA does not authorize tariffs.
  • 24 Feb 202610% Section 122 surcharge starts, for up to 150 days.
  • 7 May 2026Trade court finds Section 122 tariffs unlawful for three plaintiffs. Stayed on appeal.
  • 24 Jul 2026Section 122 expires. Forced-labor Section 301 tariffs start on 60 economies.
  • 6 Oct 2026CBP opens Phase 3 of IEEPA refunds (CAPE).

How we got here

In Learning Resources, Inc. v. Trump (20 February 2026) the Supreme Court held 6–3 that the International Emergency Economic Powers Act does not authorize tariffs. That same day the President invoked Section 122 of the Trade Act of 1974, which allows a temporary surcharge of up to 15% for 150 days. A 10% surcharge ran from 24 February to 24 July.

When it expired, USTR put new Section 301 tariffs in place on 60 economies. USTR based them on findings about forced-labor import enforcement.

USTR set 10% for countries that committed to adopting and enforcing forced-labor import bans, and 12.5% for those that did not. A 12.5% rate can drop to 10% if a country adopts a ban USTR considers potentially effective.

What's exempt

  • Goods covered by the Section 232 programs listed in the notice.
  • Canadian and Mexican goods entered duty-free under USMCA.
  • About 2,120 tariff codes on a universal exemption list (Annex II, Part A). Some apply only to specific end uses, such as civil aircraft or pharmaceuticals. Thirteen economies have their own extra lists, but China is not one of them.

Importers challenged the tariffs at the Court of International Trade on 24 July, and the case is pending.

What it means for components

For Chinese-origin parts, the 12.5% is added on top of existing Section 301 duties. A part on Section 301 List 3 now typically pays its MFN rate, plus 25%, plus 12.5%. Parts made in Japan, Taiwan or the EU are charged a combined rate, so a reducer or encoder from those origins often costs less to land than the same part from China. The country of origin shown on your commercial invoice now decides more of the landed cost than it used to.

If you paid IEEPA duties in 2025–26

CBP refunds IEEPA duties through its CAPE process. By mid-September, CBP reported about $122 billion, including interest, certified and sent to Treasury for payment. About $1.3 billion was on hold because importers had not filed ACH banking details. Phase 3, which opened on 6 October, lets eligible plaintiffs claim refunds on finally liquidated entries that are subject to court-ordered reliquidation. If your broker filed entries for you, ask them about your CAPE declaration status.
