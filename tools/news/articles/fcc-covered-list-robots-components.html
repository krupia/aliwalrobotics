Two FCC rules, both now in force

The FCC Covered List doesn't set a duty. It stops the FCC from issuing new equipment authorizations, and a radio-equipped or digital device generally needs one before it can be marketed or sold in the US. Two recent changes bring the list much closer to robotics and data-capture hardware.

1. Foreign-produced mobile robots (since 28 July 2026). The FCC's Public Safety and Homeland Security Bureau added foreign-produced "advanced robotic devices" to the Covered List in Public Notice DA 26-786. A device is covered only if it meets every part of the definition. It must:

  • be a mechanical mobile device that can move on the ground, such as an autonomous mobile robot, humanoid or quadruped
  • operate at a distance from a human operator, based on commands or sensor data
  • weigh more than 4.4 pounds, including any ground or docking station
  • contain a sensor that perceives its environment, a wired or wireless network connection of at least 200 kbps, and software (including firmware and AI model weights) that controls navigation, perception, data collection or remote command

The definition excludes connected vehicles, rail vehicles, drones, underwater vehicles, FDA-regulated medical devices such as surgical robots, and fixed industrial robots such as SCARA arms. Models authorized before the listing can still be imported, sold and used. A device counts as a domestic end product, and falls outside the listing, if US-made components exceed 65% of its value, rising to 70% in 2029. Small batches of unauthorized prototypes may be imported for development or testing only.

2. Chips and modules from Covered List companies (since 13 October 2026). Under the FCC's Third Report and Order, adopted on 22 July, the FCC won't authorize a device that contains a "logic-bearing hardware component" made by a Covered List entity, if the finished device would itself be barred had that entity made it. The FCC defines the term broadly: a device, system, module, sub-assembly, integrated circuit or other physical component that uses digital techniques or radio-frequency energy to process data. The rule applies to new applications. It generally doesn't affect equipment already authorized, unless an application is changed to add or swap in a covered component.

What happened on 22 October

  • First component enforcement. The FCC's Office of Engineering and Technology blocked a dashboard camera and a wireless video transmitter from being marketed or sold in the US. Both contained a chip from HiSilicon, a Huawei subsidiary, that HiSilicon advertises as "designed for the surveillance market." The FCC did not name the device makers. Chairman Brendan Carr said the FCC will keep enforcing the rules.
  • First robotics hardware waiver. The FCC let Intel make certain hardware changes to automated guided vehicles in its own factories for one year (DA 26-1146). Intel committed that the changes won't add capabilities, won't improve or alter the vehicles' radio-frequency characteristics and won't replace a US-made part with a foreign one. Wiley describes it as the first waiver that allows hardware changes to robots already in the field. A separate blanket waiver (DA 26-789) covers certain software and firmware updates.

Conditional approvals are being granted

A producer can ask the Department of War for a "Conditional Approval" that exempts a device or product class. The application covers corporate structure, supply chain and a US manufacturing or onshoring plan. FCC public notices from 2 to 23 October list approvals for robotic devices from Matic Robots, Volley Automation, Vulcan Robotics, Haystack Robotics and T-Robotics (an entire class of automated guided vehicles), as well as approvals for drones, routers and power inverters.

On 30 October the FCC announced another round of conditional exemptions, covering specified foreign-produced drones, advanced robotic devices (including automated material-handling systems) and power inverters. The National Law Review alert by Bradley says the round rests on conditional Department of War approvals for nine devices. It did not name them. The exemptions depend on the companies following their onshoring plans and on updated product vetting. Approval can be ended if a company fails to follow its plan or makes a false statement in its application.

What it means for components and data-collection devices

  • Loose components aren't on the robotics listing. The 28 July listing covers finished mobile robots. Actuators, reducers, motors and encoders bought as parts aren't covered by it. But if you build or import a complete mobile robot, the origin of the finished device and its parts now affects whether it can get FCC authorization.
  • Check the chips in cameras and radios. The component rule is the one that reaches data-capture hardware. Cameras, video transmitters, wireless modules and compute boards that use a chip or module from a Covered List company can't get new authorizations. Ask suppliers which SoC and radio module each device uses, and who makes them.
  • Ask for the FCC ID. A device authorized before 13 October generally isn't affected by the component rule. Online marketplaces will have to show valid FCC IDs on listings from 1 March 2027 or 1 June 2027, depending on how they operate.

For cameras, headsets, radios and compute modules, we confirm the FCC ID and the main chip and module makers before quoting.
